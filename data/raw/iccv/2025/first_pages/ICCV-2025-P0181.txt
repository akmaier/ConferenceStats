A major challenge in Low-Light Image Enhancement
(LLIE) is its ill-posed nature: low-light images often lack
sufficient information to align with normal-light ones (e.g.,
not all training data can be fully fitted to the ground truth).
Numerous studies have attempted to bridge the gap between
low- and normal-light data by introducing effective additional information, which is called “references” in this paper. However, existing methods overlook the valuable references hidden within the training dataset itself. In this
work, we propose a novel LLIE strategy that simultaneously
learns image-specific features by neural networks while formulating effective common features from the training data
as the reference. These common features are correlated
with the samples that are not fully fitted by the LLIE network
itself, and they are represented as a set of Learnable Feature
Patches and Vectors (LFPVs) in the hidden feature space.
LFPVs are updated through two mechanisms: the sampleupdater, which extracts useful features from training samples to refine LFPVs, and the mutual-updater, which propagates information across LFPVs to mutually update them.
LFPVs can be adaptively aligned with image-specific features via our designed query-and-fusion procedure, boosting the LLIE performance. Our proposed method can be
integrated into any LLIE framework, improving both enhancement quality and downstream task performance. Extensive experiments on various benchmarks demonstrate the
effectiveness of our approach.

Current strategies to improve LLIE methods can be categorized into three kinds. The first focuses on designing
more effective network architectures, such as transitioning
from CNNs to Transformer-based structures [1, 29, 35].
However, network capacity has its limits, and parameter efficiency is crucial in practice. A second approach is expanding the training dataset [17, 23, 33]. However, collecting
strictly paired data across devices is challenging, and poorquality data may degrade results. The third direction is identifying effective “references” (additional resources besides
network inputs) that can assist LLIE.
The selection of an appropriate reference is complex,
and current works mainly adopt three categories. The first
kind involves data collected from other devices, such as
event cameras [12, 15]. However, capturing such data requires additional devices, which is impractical for some
real-world applications, such as mobile imaging. The second category introduces multi-modal predictions as the reference [27, 30], including high- and mid-level scene parsing, such as segmentation or depth. While some effects have
been proven, these predictions are not always accurate. As
a result, improvements can be inconsistent.
