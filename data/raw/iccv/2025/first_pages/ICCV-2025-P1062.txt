Abstract
Line drawing colorization is a critical step in the celanimation industry, where artists use a paint bucket tool
to apply RGB values to segments based on a character’s
color design sheet. Current automated methods predominantly focus on consecutive frame colorization, using a single adjacent frame as a reference. These approaches often
face two major challenges: inaccurate segment colorization
due to significant deformations between the target and reference frames, and incomplete information in a single frame
that prevents finding suitable reference segments, leading to
poor color accuracy. To address these challenges, we propose a novel colorization framework that integrates both
temporal and structural information. Using multiple reference keyframes, our method effectively captures temporal
information across frames, enhancing the accuracy of colorization for transitional frames. In addition, we leverage
structural information through a matching-based approach
that ensures precise segment alignment across frames. This
combination of temporal awareness through multi-frame
references and structural alignment improves colorization
robustness, even in scenarios with large motion and deformations. Our method outperforms existing techniques,
demonstrating superior colorization accuracy and consistency in industrial cel-animation workflows.

Considerable work is needed to achieve accurate colorization for every frame, even with these tools’ support. Using

segments, which result in a considerable workload due to
the multitude of frames in an animation sequence.
To improve the efficiency of line art colorization, various
studies have investigated algorithms for pixel-level RGB
value estimation to convert sketches to colored images, such
as AniDoc [15], ColorFlow [31], and LVCD [7]. These
methods are often capable of producing visually appealing
colored images that showcase impressive perceptual quality.
However, they face significant limitations when it comes to
integration into the industrial cel animation pipeline. The
primary issue lies in the conflict between the industrial requirements of cel animation and the inherent nature of images generated by pixel-level estimation.In the field of cel
animation, industry standards require that, during the colorization process, each segment be filled with a single, consistent color according to established color charts. However, this type of technique, which aims to estimate the
RBG value at the pixel level, naturally leads to flickering
and color bleeding. This characteristic renders them inappropriate for the requirements of the animation industry [5].
