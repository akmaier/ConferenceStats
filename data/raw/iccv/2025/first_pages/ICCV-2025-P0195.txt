Removing Out-of-Focus Reflective Flares via Color Alignment
Fengbo Lan Chang Wen Chen
The Hong Kong Polytechnic University

Abstract
Reflective flares are common artifacts in photography that
degrade image quality, introducing in-focus flares, which
appear as bright, regular spot patterns, and out-of-focus
flares, which are diffuse and semi-transparent, obscuring the underlying scene. While previous methods have
achieved some success in removing in-focus flares, they
struggle with the diffuse nature of out-of-focus flares. The
lack of an out-of-focus flare dataset has further hindered
the development of effective flare removal models. In this
work, we construct a large-scale out-of-focus flare dataset
generated with Blender. We propose to use a color alignment approach on diffusion models to address the challenges of out-of-focus reflective flare removal. Rather than
fully reconstructing flare-affected regions, our method adjusts the color distribution to reduce artifact visibility while
preserving image content. Specifically, we apply a differentiable histogram loss on the diffusion model, which is derived from the Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD), to effectively
align color distributions. The proposed approach, trained
exclusively on synthetic data, outperforms existing methods
on both synthetic and real-world data, demonstrating improved performance in flare removal.

1. Introduction
Reflective flares [11, 13, 28] are a common challenge in
photography, caused by light reflecting between lens elements in multi-element lens systems [4, 14, 18, 32]. These
internal reflections can produce geometric shapes such as
concentric rings or polygons, depending on the lens design and the position of the light source, as illustrated in
Fig. 1. Reflective flares are especially noticeable when a
bright light source is near the optical axis and when the lens
system contains multiple elements. Their appearance varies
based on different reflection paths: in-focus flares appear as
bright, regular spot patterns, while out-of-focus flares are
diffuse and semi-transparent, often covering large areas and
blending into the background.
In mobile device photography, reflective flare is more

pronounced due to cost and weight constraints on optical
systems. Mobile camera lenses are typically made from
plastic rather than high-quality glass, which offers better optical performance. Additionally, mobile devices often lack
advanced coating techniques, such as anti-reflective (AR)
coatings [3], which effectively reduce flare but are costly to
implement. As shown in Fig. 1, even flagship models suffer
from flare artifacts caused by bright light sources like the
sun or artificial lights, obscuring the underlying scene and
degrading image quality.
