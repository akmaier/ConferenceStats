Proof of Eq. 2: Wasserstein Distance

When given two multivariate normal distributions q, p with mean vectors µq , µp
and covariance matrices Σq , Σp , the Wasserstein distance W from [8] is
  \label {eq: wass} \mathcal {W}[\mathrm {q}, \mathrm {p}] = \left \|\boldsymbol {\mu }_{\mathrm {q}}-\boldsymbol {\mu }_{\mathrm {p}}\right \|^2+\operatorname {tr}\left (\boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {q}}\right )+\operatorname {tr}\left (\boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {p}}\right ) -2 \operatorname {tr}\left (\left (\boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {q}} \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {p}}\right )^{1 / 2}\right ), 

(8)

where tr(·) is the trace operator. This expression is also called Fréchet distance.
Another common expression for the W from [12, 27] is written as
  \mathcal {W}[\mathrm {q}, \mathrm {p}] = \left \|\boldsymbol {\mu }_{\mathrm {q}}-\boldsymbol {\mu }_{\mathrm {p}}\right \|^2+\operatorname {tr}\left (\boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {q}}\right )+\operatorname {tr}\left (\boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {p}}\right ) -2\operatorname {tr}\left (\left (\boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {q}}^{1 / 2} \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {p}} \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {q}}^{1 / 2}\right )^{1 / 2}\right ). \label {eq: wass2} 

(9)

The equivalence between (8) and (9) can be proved by noting that:
  \operatorname {tr}\left (\left (\boldsymbol {\Sigma }_p^{1 / 2} \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_q \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_p^{1 / 2}\right )^{1 / 2}\right ) &=\operatorname {tr}\left (\left (\boldsymbol {\Sigma }_p^{1 / 2} \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_q \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_p^{1 / 2}\right )^{1 / 2} \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_p^{-1 / 2} \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_p^{1 / 2}\right ) \nonumber \\ & =\operatorname {tr}\left (\boldsymbol {\Sigma }_p^{1 / 2}\left (\boldsymbol {\Sigma }_p^{1 / 2} \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_q \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_p^{1 / 2}\right )^{1 / 2} \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_p^{-1 / 2}\right ).
(10)
Then we have

  \left (\boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {p}}^{1 / 2}\left (\boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {p}}^{1 / 2} \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {q}} \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {p}}^{1 / 2}\right )^{1 / 2} \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {p}}^{-1 / 2}\right )^2=\boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {p}} \boldsymbol {\Sigma }_{\mathrm {q}} \text {.} 
(11)

Therefore, (8) and (9) are equivalent.
For the convenience of computation, we choose the form of (8). If we assume
a target distribution is a standard multivariate normal distribution N (0, Id ) in
Rd , then (8) becomes,
