Experimental Analysis

1.1

More Comparison with the State-of-the-art Methods

Training details. We observed that the results of Synapse [10] in [15] did
not fully converge. Hence, we retrained for 17k iterations to ensure convergence
of all methods. For MagicNet [5], we trained for 34k iterations as it required
more iterations to reach convergence. Our proposed method, along with ablation
studies and analysis, were all trained for 17k iterations. The settings for AMOS
are the same as Synapse. For ACDC dataset, we add SS-Net [20] and BCP [1].
MagicNet [5] is not applied for ACDC as it’s a 3D method. We applied our GA
loss to the official code of [1, 12, 20] while keeping all other parameters the same
as the released code.
Comparison with the state-of-the-arts. The results of 10% labeled Synapse
dataset are presented in Table 1. Our GA loss is applied to eight SOTA SSL segmentation methods. Significant improvements can be seen for all eight methods:
MT (↑ 9.28%, 27.85% to GA-MT with 37.13%), UA-MT (↑ 10.54%), RDrop (↑
13.75%), CPS (↑ 12.33%), DePL (↑ 8.88%) and MagicNet (↑ 3.07%). Despite
using only two labeled cases, our method demonstrates remarkable superiority
in tail classes (small organs), avoiding situations of terrible failing prediction on
all methods.
Furthermore, GA loss surpasses all SOTA class-imbalanced designs. Our
methods achieve the best results within the same framework. Specifically, our
GA-MT outperforms Basak et al .’s method (↑ 2.72%), which utilized a class-wise
sampling strategy on MT. Additionally, our GA-CPS demonstrated superior performance compared to all CPS-based class-imbalanced designs (Adsh, CReST,
SimiS, CLD, and DHC), surpassing the best DHC by 5.70% among them. Similar
conclusions can be drawn from the 2% labeled AMOS dataset in Table 2.
