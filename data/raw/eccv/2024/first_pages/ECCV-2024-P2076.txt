Algorithm-1 description

The Douglas-Rachford (DR) splitting technique is a powerful method used for
solving optimization problems, especially when the objective function can be
split into two parts. This method has been used in various domains, including
signal processing, image reconstruction, and now, as we propose, in adversarial training for enhancing model robustness. DR splitting minimizes the sum of
two functions "\protect \underset  {x}{\min }~ f(x) + g(x)", where x represents the variables to be optimized, and f and g are two convex functions. This technique employs a reflection
step to project the current estimate xk across the function (f ) and computing
x_{ref}=2\cdot \text {prox}^f (x^k)-x^k  , with ‘prox’ indicating the proximal operator. Subsequently, a projection step forces the reflected point xref onto the other function
(g), as x^{k+1}= \text {prox}^g (x_{ref}), balancing the optimization towards convergence. Indeed, the goal of DR splitting is to find a balance between these two functions,
optimizing them together (see, Theorem 25.2 in [1]).
However, in the context of adversarial training, we align the DR splitting
method with the process of enhancing model robustness by considering the minimization of adversarial loss (function f ) and applying regularization (function
g) to control model complexity. The reflection step (Eq.(8)) involves updating
model parameters (θ(k) ) based on adversarial examples x′(k) . The objective is
to reduce the loss L(f (x′(k) , θ(k) ), y), reflecting the model’s capacity to counter
adversarial perturbations. This step directly combats adversarial vulnerabilities
by adjusting the model to minimize the impact of adversarial examples, thus
targeting the reduction of adversarial loss. Following the reflection, the model
incorporates an enforcement/regularization step (Eq.(9) and Eq.(10)) to ensure
training stability. This is achieved by combining the intermediate updates (C k )
with a regularization component to enforce model stability. The importance of
DR splitting in mitigating CO lies in continually balancing the model’s responses
to both adversarial and benign examples. Preventing the model from leaning too
much towards one side and losing overall effectiveness.
