Abstract
Despite recent advances in deep texture recognition, existing methods still lack representational diversity and struggle to capture and preserve discriminative cues across
stages of representation hierarchies. Moreover, many rely
on loss formulations that prioritize recognition accuracy
while overlooking spatial coherence and statistical consistency in the feature space. To address these issues, we
propose three key innovations: (1) Stochastic Local Texture Masking (SLTM), a regularization strategy that randomly occludes small texture patches to promote the learning of broader spatial and contextual dependencies; (2) the
Chebyshev Attention Depth Permutation Texture Network
(CAPTN), a novel architecture that learns expressive and
persistent Latent Texture Attribute (LTA) representations.
CAPTN integrates a Texture Frequency Attention (TFA)
module that generates Latent Texture Attributes (LTAs)
and enables frequency-aware interpretability, a Dual Depth
Permutation (D2 P) module to expose complementary channel adjacency patterns, and Learnable Chebyshev Polynomials (LCPs) to model high-order orderless LTA transformations via recursive Chebyshev basis expansion; and
(3) a Latent Texture Attribute Loss that jointly optimizes
classification accuracy, statistical alignment, and spatial
fidelity. CAPTN supports end-to-end training without relying on fine-tuned CNN backbones and achieves state-ofthe-art performance on several texture and material recognition benchmarks. (Code: https://github.com/
RavishankarEvani/CAPTN )

With the rise of data-driven approaches, modern deep
learning methods have largely supplanted handcrafted descriptors by offering more expressive and hierarchical feature representations. Despite this progress, deep models
still encounter challenges when applied to texture recognition. In particular, successive convolution and pooling operations can suppress or discard fine-grained texture cues,
limiting the network’s ability to retain localized and discriminative information. Furthermore, textures often exhibit overlapping or perceptually similar traits [37], which
contribute to inter-class confusion by blurring the boundaries between distinct texture categories. For instance, textures labeled as “stratified” might closely resemble those labeled “cracked” or “zigzagged,” making it difficult to consistently assign the correct class. In addition, intra-class
variability arises from variations within the same category
due to differences in lighting, scale, deformation, or inherent surface irregularities.
The concept of texture attributes [10, 37, 39] has been
shown to play a crucial role in representing perceptually similar textures. Latent texture attributes [10] refer
to the underlying semantic components of visual texture
attributes, where each latent texture attribute may correspond to one or more perceptual texture attributes. Effectively manipulating and transforming these latent attributes
can enhance texture discrimination.
