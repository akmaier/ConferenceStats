For example, for a complex causal network, the conventional
Design of Experiments (DOE) principles often fail to provide useful guidance to network intervention design (Cox

The primary goal of traditional DOE is to ascertain
whether the treatment variable influences the outcome variable, and if so, to what extent. However, it does not offer help
on how to select treatment variables. When a bipartite separation of variables is assumed, many previous studies were
carried out under the assumption that limited interventions
can reveal causality given time-ordered information. However, there are numerous other situations where such restrictions are unwarranted (e.g., when multiple causal pathways
exist between treatment and outcome variables).
A more comprehensive framework for modeling causal
structures – causal Bayes nets – has been developed in computer science and philosophy fields (Pearl 2000; Spirtes,
Glymour, and Scheines 2000). Unlike the bipartite structure assumed in DOE, causal Bayes nets accommodate general graphical networks (though this discussion is limited
to acyclic networks). They represent causal relationships
among variables by a DAG and a probability distribution
over the variables in the DAG (Koller and Friedman 2009).
This framework calls for new principles for intervention experimental designs, and it involves setting new design parameters such as selecting intervention and outcome variables, determining the type of intervention (structural or
parametric), and the level of perturbation for each intervention variable. Moreover, the diversity of interventions should
be planned to cover different aspects or levels of the variables of interest, including combinatorial interventions to
explore joint effects and interactions while not exceeding
cost limitations. Ensuring an adequate sample size is also
a crucial design parameter for achieving sufficient statistical
power, improving generalizability, and counting variations
in outcome measures. The main focus in recent years has
been on the number of intervention experiments required to
reveal a causal structure under certain assumptions, as well
as deciding on which variables are to be intervened on in
each experiment. The methodologies for determining other
design parameters remain open problems.

Causal discovery is essential across various scientific fields
to uncover causal structures within data. Traditional methods
relying on observational data have limitations due to variable
confounding. This paper presents an optimization-based approach using integer programming (IP) to design minimal intervention sets that ensure causal structure identifiability. Our
method provides exact and modular solutions, adaptable to
different experimental settings and constraints. We demonstrate its effectiveness through comparative analysis across
different settings to demonstrate its applicability and robustness.

Introduction
Causal discovery is a crucial endeavor across many scientific
fields. It focuses on revealing the causal structures within
data. Generally, causal discovery can be carried out through
one of two data collection approaches – observational databased discovery and interventional or experimental databased discovery. The majority of past research employs observational methods, such as those using conditional independence tests, to offer valuable causal structure insights.
However, these methods have significant limitations, as they
often face challenges from confounding variables and their
insufficiency of conclusively determining causality (Pearl
2009; Spirtes, Glymour, and Scheines 2000). Observationbased causal discovery can at most reveal a Directed Acyclic
Graph (DAG) up to its Markov Equivalence Class (MEC)
level, which means that there could be multiple different
causal structures equivalent to fitting to the conditional independence revealed by the observational data alone (Verma
and Pearl 1990).
The experimental approach, in contrast, provides a robust framework for discovering causal relationships by systematically manipulating variables and observing the effects
(Fisher 1935). Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) is often
considered the gold standard in this context, offering clear
evidence of causality by isolating the effects of specific interventions (Rosenbaum 2002). However, traditional experimental designs presume a bipartite separation of variables
